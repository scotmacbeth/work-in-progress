## Deep dive: MO 431721 ("A new (?) way of composing monads", Simon Henry, Oct 2022)

**Setup as originally posed by Henry.** Two monads S, T. Classical distributive law: l: TS → ST satisfying the usual 4 hexagon axioms (unit-S, unit-T, mult-S, mult-T compatibility), used to build the monad structure on ST by the standard formula μ_{ST} = (μ_S μ_T)∘(S l T), η_{ST} = ε_S ε_T.

Henry's situation instead has a map l: TS → ST with:
1. **l is an isomorphism** (invertible).
2. l satisfies only **2 of the 4** distributive-law axioms: compatibility with the **unit of T** and with the **multiplication of T**. (In nLab terms: l is "a distributive law of the monad T on the underlying endofunctor of S" — it's a genuine T-side distributive law, but says nothing about S's structure.)
3. l satisfies **one additional, strictly weaker axiom** in place of compatibility with μ_S: the two composites SSTS → ST,
   `SSTS --μ_S T S--> STS --l⁻¹S--> TSS --Tμ_S--> TS --l--> ST`
   and
   `SSTS --Sl⁻¹S--> STSS --STμ_S--> STS --Sl--> SST --μ_S T--> ST`
   are equal (this is *not* the standard hexagon for μ_S; it uses l⁻¹ mixed in).

Critically, the **multiplication built on ST is NOT the classical distributive-law formula**. Instead:
`μ_{ST} = (Sμ_T)∘(l⁻¹T)∘(Tμ_S T)∘(l⁻¹ST) : (ST)(ST) → ST`
i.e. it routes through l⁻¹ twice, not just S l T once. Henry notes explicitly that *if* l happened to be a genuine (all-4-axiom) invertible distributive law, this alternate formula would coincide with the classical one — but in general, absent axiom (μ_S)-compatibility, it's a genuinely different construction.

**Concrete example (monoids).** S = M any monoid, T = L(M) = finite lists over M with concatenation. l: L(M)×M → M×L(M) sends `((a_1,...,a_k), m) ↦ (a_1, (a_2,...,a_k,m))` and `(∅, a) ↦ (a, ∅)`. This satisfies (1)-(3) but is *not* a distributive law (fails unit-S and mult-S compatibility). The resulting monoid on M×L(M) is L⁺(M), nonempty lists over M with multiplication that concatenates but multiplies the adjoining elements in M.

**Henry's own resolution (in his answer, prompted by comments from მამუკა ჯიბლაძე and varkor about wreaths, Formal Theory of Monads II §3).** He shows the construction is **not a genuinely new mechanism**: define
`α = l ∘ (Tμ_S) ∘ (l⁻¹S) ∘ (ε_S TS) : TS → ST`.
He claims (diagram chase, not fully spelled out in the post) that **α is an honest distributive law** (all 4 axioms), and that the monad structure on ST built from l via his alternate formula equals the monad structure built from α via the *classical* distributive-law formula. Note: **α is generally NOT invertible**, even though l was. Conclusion in his own words: *"This is 'just' a distributive law with some special additional property, but I was using the wrong function TS → ST."*

**The monoid-theoretic picture he gives (most illuminating for MacBeth's ZS question).** A classical distributive law TS→ST between monoid-monads corresponds to a monoid M containing S, T as submonoids with **unique factorization** — every element of M written uniquely as st (this is precisely the Zappa-Szép / exact-factorization picture). Henry's weld generalizes this: it corresponds to **two different inclusions i, j: T → M** (not one!) plus a submonoid S ⊆ M, such that every element of M is uniquely both j(t)s and s'i(t'). l is the bijection sending (t,s) ↦ (s',t') where j(t)s = s'i(t'). The failure of l to respect μ_S/η_S is exactly measured by i ≠ j: i, j are recovered as T→ST via ε_S T and via T→TS→ST (through l), and **l compatible with η_S ⟺ i=j**, which is exactly the ordinary ZS/exact-factorization situation.

**Answer to MacBeth's question: is this a degenerate ZS special case, a genuinely different weld, or a third mechanism?**
- At the level of the **final monad structure produced**, it is provably **not a new mechanism**: Henry constructs an explicit (generally non-invertible) classical distributive law α that reproduces exactly the same monad on ST. So anything buildable via his "weak invertible weld" is also buildable via an ordinary (possibly non-invertible) Beck distributive law / ordinary ZS-type exact factorization — it does *not* extend the classical theory's reach for what final composite monads/monoids are constructible.
- At the level of the **input data** (the weld map itself), it genuinely **is a different, more general structure** than a plain ZS product: a plain ZS/exact-factorization needs a *single* pair of submonoid inclusions S,T↪M with l respecting both units; Henry's version needs **two inclusions i≠j of T** into M, related by l but not required to coincide. So it sits strictly between "arbitrary invertible map TS→ST" and "genuine ZS/distributive law" — a *twisted* or *skew* version parametrized by the discrepancy i vs j, but this extra data is always secretly reducible/repackageable as (a possibly non-invertible) ordinary distributive law once you compose in α. Varkor's comment flags **wreaths** (Street, "Formal theory of monads II" §3) as the standard further generalization of distributive-law monad-composition beyond simple DLs — worth checking against MacBeth's own weld mechanism (two-sided ZS/H² weld and T_M-over-M Street lifting) since wreaths are exactly Street-lifting-flavored. No further discussion of wreaths appears in the thread beyond the one-line pointer.
- **Bottom line for MacBeth:** Henry's mechanism is a *third-looking but ultimately reducible* weld — not literally the same combinatorics as a plain ZS product (two embeddings vs one), but at the monad-output level it collapses back into ordinary distributive-law theory. This is a useful cleared-neighbour: it does NOT anticipate MacBeth's two-sided ZS/H² weld or T_M-over-M Street lifting (those are about fibred/Street-style lifting over a base, not monoid-inside-M factorizations), but the "wreath" pointer from varkor is worth a direct check against Street's Formal Theory of Monads II §3 if not already done.

## Deep dive: MO 499885 ("Is the free monad 2-monad cartesian?", asked by mattecapu Sep 2025, answered by Kevin Carlson)

**Question.** For K a locally finitely presentable 2-category, finitary monads on K are monadic over finitary endofunctors; let (F,i,m) be the free-monad 2-monad. Does F preserve pullbacks? Do i (unit) and m (multiplication) have naturality squares that are pullbacks (i.e. are they cartesian natural transformations)? Asker notes the plausibility ("free monoid"-like monads are usually cartesian) but can't prove it for general K, and offers a fallback weaker question (cartesianity restricted to discrete fibrations).

**Kevin Carlson's answer: NO**, refuted by an explicit counterexample on Set (as a "trivially 2-dimensional" lfp 2-category, so the question reduces to ordinary free monads on finitary Set-endofunctors). For the representable endofunctor y^A, the free monad Fy^A(1) = rooted A-ary trees. Key computation: Fy^0(1) = 2 (height-0 and height-1 nullary trees). The unique maps y^A → y^0 induce Fy^A(1) → Fy^0(1) collapsing everything but the height-0 tree to the height-1 tree. Pullback of y^A → y^0 ← y^B is y^{A+B}, so cartesianity of F at this cospan would require: {positive-height (A+B)-trees} ≅ {positive-height A-trees} × {positive-height B-trees}. Carlson gives an explicit counterexample with A={1,2}, B={3,4}: two distinct height-3 4-ary trees both map to the same pair of binary trees under the induced maps — **not a pullback**, so **F does not preserve pullbacks** and hence the free monad functor is **not cartesian**.
Also: the **unit i is not cartesian** — for f:B→A, y^f(1)=id_1 but Fy^f(1) is far from injective in general, so the naturality square for the unit at y^f can't be a pullback (this uses the same tree-collapsing structure). Carlson explicitly says he hasn't worked out the multiplication m's cartesianity.
Bonus remark: since composition products of endofunctors are only closed on one side, the naive "Σ_n H^{∘n}" formula for the free monad doesn't even hold set-theoretically for H = y^A on Set — a caveat about the very premise of the question.

This is consistent with (and gives an independent, very concrete witness for) MacBeth's own finding that the cartesian/∏-Mendler ladder is strict and that "free"-flavored constructions tend to sit at higher, non-cartesian rungs unless special conditions hold — worth citing as prior art / sanity check if MacBeth ever states "free monads are typically not cartesian" in the book.
