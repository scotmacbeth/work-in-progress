Dear Alastair,

First, your bet. Yesterday I asked you to guess the COPRODUCT of two containers — "either a
C or a D" — before computing. Here is the answer, and it is the interesting kind of answer,
because the flip *sits this one out*.

    Shapes of C + D:   S + T          (a shape of C, OR a shape of D — pick a side)
    Positions at inl s:   P(s)        (just C's positions)
    Positions at inr t:   Q(t)        (just D's positions)

No sum-of-positions, no flip. And the reason is exactly yesterday's reflex, run in reverse.
The product *combined* two structures over the same data, so its positions had to accommodate
both at once — and "both at once, contravariantly" turned into a sum. The coproduct never puts
two structures over the same data; it picks one. Nothing is combined, so nothing gets flipped.
If your guess said "the flip strikes again" — this is the good kind of wrong, because now you
know precisely *which feature* summons the flip: combination, not choice. (Check: [[C + D]] X =
[[C]] X + [[D]] X. A sum of functors, on the nose.)

Now — composition. I have made you wait two days for it, and here is why it was worth waiting.

Containers are functors, and functors *compose*. So there ought to be a container C ◁ D whose
extension is [[C]] after [[D]]:

    [[ C ◁ D ]] X  =  [[C]] ( [[D]] X )

Let us just *demand* that and see what shapes and positions we are forced into. Write C = S ◁ P
and D = T ◁ Q. A point of [[C]]([[D]] X) is: a C-shape s, and for each C-position p ∈ P(s), a
point of [[D]] X — that is, a D-shape together with an X-labelling of its positions. So to name
a shape of the composite, *before* any X gets involved, you must give:

    a C-shape s,   and for every C-position p ∈ P(s),   a D-shape.

Read that slowly, because you have just built a **tree of depth two**. The C-shape is the root.
Its positions P(s) are the branches. And hanging off the end of every branch is a D-shape — a
little cluster of leaves. A shape of C ◁ D is not a pair; it is a *two-storey building*:

    Shapes of C ◁ D:   Sum_{s in S} ( P(s) -> T )

"An outer shape, plus an inner shape chosen at each outer position." That `P(s) -> T` is the
assignment of a D-shape to every branch. Now, where can you put an element of X in this
building? You must walk *down*: choose an outer position p ∈ P(s) — pick a branch — and then
choose an inner position in the D-shape hanging there. A position is a **path of length two**:

    Positions at (s, f):   Sum_{p in P(s)} Q( f(p) )

An outer step, then an inner step. And — watch the reflex one more time — that is a SUM over
the outer positions of the inner position-sets, which is exactly what the exponential law needs
so that [[C ◁ D]] X unfolds to [[C]]([[D]] X). Sum in the positions, nesting in the data. The
same law as Day 2, now doing structural work: it is what makes "a labelling of the whole tree"
equal to "an outer labelling of inner-labelled subtrees."

One concrete case. Let C = D = Lists. A shape of Lists ◁ Lists is: a length n (the outer list),
and for each of its n slots, another length (the length of the inner list there). A list of
lists — a ragged table. Its positions: pick an outer slot, then an inner slot. Pick a row, then
a cell. Exactly the cells of the ragged table, and nothing else. Count them and they are all
there. Composition of the list container is *nesting of lists*, and the arithmetic of positions
is just "which cell."

Here is the thing I most want you to carry away, because it is the seed of everything that
follows. **Composition of containers is the growing of trees.** C ◁ D is depth two. C ◁ C ◁ C is
depth three. Iterate, and the shapes become deeper and deeper trees; the positions become longer
and longer paths from the root. Every question about *repeated* structure — a list of lists of
lists, a folder of folders, a process that spawns processes — is a question about ◁ applied to
itself. And the moment you ask "when can I *flatten* one of these trees — collapse a path-of-two
into a single step, consistently, at every node" — you are asking whether the container carries
the structure of a **category**. That is where I am taking you next: the containers that know how
to compose their own positions. They are called *directed*, and they are secretly small
categories wearing a costume. But that is tomorrow.
